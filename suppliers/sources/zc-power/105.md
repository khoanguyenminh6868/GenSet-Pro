What Is a Standby Generator?
What Is a Standby Generator?
The standby generator functions as a backup power system which remains permanently installed to provide automatic electricity during utility power outages. The operation of standby generators functions automatically because their integrated transfer switching systems detect power outages and restore electricity within seconds unlike portable generators which need human operators to set up their power systems.
Core Characteristics
Standby generators differ from other power solutions in several key aspects:
Permanent Installation : Hard-wired into building electrical systems with dedicated fuel supplies
Automatic Operation : Requires no human intervention during power failures
Limited Runtime Design : Engineered for emergency backup, not continuous operation (typically rated for 200-500 hours annually)
Code-Compliant Construction : Built to meet NFPA 110, NEC Article 445, and applicable local requirements

The primary difference between standby generators and prime power generators exists because standby generators serve emergency situations while prime power generators provide continuous power delivery. The use of a standby-rated generator for prime power applications results in warranty cancellation and increases the possibility of equipment failure before its expected lifespan.

NFPA 110 Classification System
The National Fire Protection Association Standard 110 defines classifications that determine acceptable performance for different applications.
Type Classifications (Transfer Time):
Type 10 : Power must be restored within 10 seconds (hospitals, data centers, critical life safety)
Type 60 : Power must be restored within 60 seconds (commercial buildings, less critical applications)
Type 120 : Power must be restored within 120 seconds (non-critical standby loads)
Level Classifications (Consequences of Failure):
Level 1 : Failure could result in loss of human life or serious injuries (hospitals, emergency services)
Level 2 : Failure causes less critical impact (commercial buildings, industrial processes)
Hospital operating rooms require Type 10, Level 1 systems. Office buildings may use Type 60, Level 2. Understanding these classifications ensures your installation meets both operational needs and code requirements.
How Standby Generators Work: Automatic Transfer Systems
How Standby Generators Work: Automatic Transfer Systems
The automatic transfer switch (ATS) serves as the essential component that operates all standby generator systems. The system monitors power from the utility network and activates generator power during outages while it switches electrical supply between the utility and generator power sources.
The Automatic Transfer Switch (ATS)
The ATS performs three critical functions:
Monitoring : Continuously measures utility voltage, frequency, and stability
Control : Sends start/stop signals to the generator controller
Switching : Physically transfers load connection between power sources
The modern ATS units use microprocessor-based controllers to achieve accurate voltage and frequency measurement while providing time delay adjustments and building management system communication features.
Sequence of Automatic Operation
When utility power fails, the following sequence occurs without human intervention:
Step 1: Detection
The ATS monitors utility power continuously. When voltage drops below typically 80% of nominal, or frequency deviates beyond acceptable limits (usually ±3 Hz), the ATS declares a power failure.
Step 2: Generator Start Signal
The ATS sends a start signal to the generator controller. The generator engine begins cranking and startup sequence.
Step 3: Engine Start and Warm-up
The generator engine starts and operates without any load until it achieves its full operating temperature and its voltage and frequency get stabilized. This process takes 5-10 seconds for diesel units while some natural gas engines require more time to complete.
Step 4: Load Transfer
The ATS system will disconnect the utility source after the generator reaches its stable output which occurs at 90% rated voltage and 95% rated frequency. The transfer process operates as a “break-before-make” mechanism because it creates a temporary power loss which lasts between 50 and 100 milliseconds.
Step 5: Utility Return Monitoring
While running on generator power, the ATS continues monitoring the utility source for stable return.
Step 6: Re-transfer
When utility power returns and remains stable for a programmed time delay (typically 15-30 minutes to ensure stability), the ATS transfers the load back to utility power.
Step 7: Generator Cool-down
The generator continues running unloaded for a cool-down period (typically 3-5 minutes) before shutting down to prevent thermal damage.

Types of Standby Generators by Fuel
Types of Standby Generators by Fuel
Fuel selection significantly impacts installation costs, operating characteristics, and runtime capabilities. Each fuel type presents distinct advantages and limitations.
Natural Gas Standby Generators
Natural gas generators connect to utility gas pipelines, providing theoretically unlimited runtime as long as the gas supply remains operational.
Advantages:
Clean burning with lower emissions than diesel
Minimal fuel maintenance (no degradation or contamination concerns)
Generally lower maintenance requirements than diesel
No on-site fuel storage needed
Limitations:
Requires natural gas line availability with adequate capacity
Pipeline failures during earthquakes or disasters can disrupt fuel supply
Limited to smaller sizes (typically under 150 kW for air-cooled units)
Higher fuel consumption per kWh than diesel
Not suitable for fire pump applications (NFPA 20 requires diesel)
Best Applications: Residential backup, small commercial facilities with reliable gas service, locations prioritizing low maintenance.
Propane (LPG) Standby Generators
Propane systems use on-site storage tanks, providing fuel independence from utility services.
Advantages:
Long fuel shelf life (unlimited when properly stored)
Cleaner burning than diesel
No fuel degradation concerns
Available in areas without natural gas service
Limitations:
Requires periodic fuel delivery
Higher fuel cost per kWh than natural gas or diesel
Storage tank installation and compliance requirements
Limited to smaller sizes (typically under 50 kW)
Best Applications: Rural locations without natural gas, facilities requiring long fuel storage life.
Diesel Standby Generators
Diesel generators serve as the standard power solution for commercial and essential operations because they provide dependable performance and high power output and meet regulatory requirements.
Advantages:
Highest power density (more kW per engine displacement)
Reliable fuel storage on-site with multi-day or week capability
Preferred for fire pump applications per NFPA 20
Better transient response for motor starting
Available in all sizes (10 kW to 4,000+ kW)
Widely accepted for Type 10, Level 1 applications
Limitations:
Higher maintenance requirements than natural gas
Fuel degradation over time (requires treatment or polishing)
Emissions regulations more stringent in some jurisdictions
Louder operation without sound attenuation
Best Applications: Hospitals, data centers, fire pumps, critical industrial processes, large commercial facilities.

Sizing a Standby Generator: Step-by-Step Method
Sizing a Standby Generator: Step-by-Step Method
Proper sizing ensures your standby generator can handle required loads without overload or excessive under-loading. The system experiences nuisance tripping because of undersizing while oversizing leads to capital waste and engine problems.
Load Analysis Process
Step 1: Inventory All Loads
Create a comprehensive list of all equipment requiring backup power. Categorize as:
Life Safety : Fire pumps, egress lighting, alarm systems (code-required)
Critical Operations : Production equipment, data centers, refrigeration
General Loads : General lighting, HVAC, convenience outlets
Step 2: Calculate Running Watts
For each load, identify the continuous running power requirement. Nameplate ratings typically indicate this value. Convert all ratings to consistent units (watts or kilowatts).
Step 3: Identify Starting Surge Requirements
Electric motors require 3-7 times their running current during startup. This locked-rotor amperage (LRA) determines whether the generator can start the motor.
Common motor starting multipliers:
Standard motors: 6x running current
High-efficiency motors: 5x running current
Fire pumps (across-the-line): 6-7x running current
Fire pumps (soft-start): 3-4x running current
Step 4: Apply Diversity Factors
Not all loads operate simultaneously. Diversity factors account for this:
Lighting : 90% (most lights on during outage)
HVAC : 75% (staggered compressor starting)
Receptacles : 50% (not all outlets fully loaded)
Motors : 80% (not all starting simultaneously)
Step 5: Add Future Growth Margin
Add 20-25% to calculated load for future expansion and calculation safety margin.

Installation Requirements and Considerations
Installation Requirements and Considerations
Standby generator installation involves electrical, fuel, structural, and regulatory components that must be coordinated for code-compliant operation.
Electrical Installation
The electrical installation connects the standby generator to the building’s power distribution system through the automatic transfer switch.
Key Components:
Service Entrance Location : ATS typically installed between utility meter and main distribution panel
Conductor Sizing : Based on generator full-load ampacity per NEC Article 445
Grounding and Bonding : Separate equipment grounding conductor, grounding electrode connections per NEC 250
Overcurrent Protection : Generator circuit breaker and output protection coordination
Load Shedding Systems:
Load shedding controls in applications with generator capacity below total building load, which first power essential circuits, automatically reduce non-essential loads when generator capacity reaches its limits.
Fuel System Installation
Natural Gas Systems:
Gas line must be sized for generator full-load consumption plus other gas appliances
Typical pressure requirements: 5-7 inches water column for residential, higher for commercial
Earthquake shut-off valves required in seismic zones
Gas utility coordination often required for large loads
Diesel Fuel Systems:
Tank sizing based on required runtime (typically 24-72 hours for critical facilities)
Double-wall tanks with leak detection for environmental compliance
Fuel transfer pumps for large tanks
Fuel treatment and polishing systems for long-term storage
Propane Systems:
Above-ground or underground tank installation
Vaporizer systems for large loads in cold climates
Capacity calculations for vaporization rates
Location and Clearance Requirements
Outdoor Installation:
Minimum clearances from combustible structures (typically 5 feet per manufacturer)
Adequate airflow for cooling (intake and exhaust clearances)
Elevation above flood levels where required
Service access on all sides requiring maintenance
Indoor Installation:
Requires ventilation for combustion air and heat rejection
Fuel supply piping compliance
Fire-rated construction requirements
Specialized exhaust systems
Noise Considerations:
The generator’s placement needs to consider noise levels which extend to the property boundaries. The distance between two points produces natural sound reduction which decreases by 6 dB for every distance increase that doubles. ZC Power’s [silent generator enclosures] achieve 65-75 dB at 7 meters for noise-sensitive installations.

Standby generator installations typically require:
Electrical Permit : Wiring, ATS installation, grounding
Building Permit : Concrete pad, enclosure, structural attachments
Fire Department Permit : Fuel storage, fire suppression
Environmental Permits : Fuel tanks, emissions (in some jurisdictions)
Final Electrical Inspection : Before activation
Code Compliance and Standards
Code Compliance and Standards
Standby generator installations must comply with multiple codes and standards. The requirements need to be understood because they protect safety and help achieve regulatory acceptance.
NFPA 110 Standard
The National Fire Protection Association Standard 110, “Standard for Emergency and Standby Power Systems,” serves as the main guide for designing and installing standby power systems.
Key Requirements:
Acceptance Testing : Full load bank testing required before system acceptance
Maintenance Requirements : Monthly testing and annual maintenance protocols
Fuel Supply : Minimum fuel capacity for required runtime
Installation Standards : Location, ventilation, and fire protection requirements
Type and Level Classifications:
Specifying the correct Type and Level ensures the system meets operational requirements:
Type 10, Level 1 : Hospital operating rooms, life support (10-second transfer, failure risks life)
Type 60, Level 1 : Hospital general loads, emergency services (60-second transfer)
Type 10, Level 2 : Data centers, critical manufacturing (10-second transfer, less critical failure)
Type 60, Level 2 : General commercial buildings (60-second transfer)

Frequently Asked Questions
What is the difference between a standby and portable generator?
Standby generators function as permanent installations which include automatic transfer switches that activate during power outages without needing operators to start them. Portable generators need operators to set them up and supply fuel and connect them at every instance of operation. Standby generators use permanent fuel connections (natural gas, diesel tank, propane), while portables typically use gasoline. Standby systems cost significantly more (5,000−5000−500,000+) versus portables (500−500−3,000) but provide seamless backup power.
How long can a standby generator run continuously?
Standby generators operate as emergency power systems with amaximum operational period of 200 to 500 hours which they can use throughout the year. The equipment can operate continuously through extended power outages but its components will experience faster deterioration. Natural gas generators can operate indefinitely as long as the pipeline supply remains active. Diesel and propane generators continue to operate until they run out of fuel. The best choice for applications that need continuous power is prime power-rated generators instead of standby-rated generators.
What size standby generator do I need?
The electrical load requirements for backup systems together with motor starting surges which reach 3 to 7 times the normal running current must be evaluated to determine system size. A typical 2,000 square foot home requires 20-30 kW. Small businesses typically need 50-150 kW. Hospitals and data centers use between 200 and 2000 kilowatts of power or more. Professional load calculations ensure proper sizing.
How much does a standby generator cost installed?
The total installed expenses start at 5000 for small residential units and reach more than 500000 for large commercial systems. The total expenses of the project consist of equipment costs which account for 50 to 70 percent while installation costs from electrical work fuel system installation and site development make up the remaining expenses. The total cost of residential installations usually falls between 10000 and 25000. The cost of commercial installations starts at 25000 for small systems and goes beyond 1000000 for large industrial standby power plants.
What is the best fuel type for standby generators?
Natural gas requires minimal upkeep while providing continuous operation for residential areas and small commercial spaces. Diesel delivers maximum dependability which makes it suitable for handling substantial weight loads and essential functions at hospitals and fire pumps. Propane is the best option for areas that lack access to natural gas in rural environments. NFPA 20 mandates diesel usage for fire pump operations while diesel remains the preferred choice for Type 10 Level 1 life safety systems.
How often should a standby generator be tested?
NFPA 110 requires monthly exercise under load for minimum 30 minutes. The facility needs to conduct annual full-load testing with load bank equipment to establish its rated capacity. The testing of transfer switches proves their ability to function automatically. The majority of jurisdictions mandate commercial establishments to maintain records of their testing activities. Monthly testing of residential systems provides operational advantages although code requirements do not mandate it. Testing procedures provide emergency preparedness assessment while detecting required maintenance tasks.
What is NFPA 110 Type 10?
NFPA 110 Type 10 classification requires restoration of power within 10 seconds of utility failure. This applies to life safety systems and critical applications including hospital operating rooms, intensive care units, and emergency services. The Type 10 standard demands that generators need to start immediately while transfer switches need to operate in a synchronized manner. Type 60 (60-second transfer) applies to less critical commercial loads. Understanding your required Type classification ensures code compliance.
Can I install my own standby generator?
Standby generator installation requires licensed electrical contractors, fuel system professionals, and permits from multiple agencies. The electrical work needs permits because it includes main service connections which require inspections. Fuel systems need to follow both fire codes and environmental regulations. Most jurisdictions prohibit DIY installation for permanently connected standby generators. Professional installation ensures safety, code compliance, and warranty validity.
How long do standby generators last?
With proper maintenance, quality standby generators last 20-30 years. Diesel engines typically outlast natural gas engines in standby service. Key factors affecting lifespan include: maintenance quality, exercise frequency, environmental conditions (corrosion, temperature), and loading (under-loading causes problems similar to overloading). Major overhauls at mid-life (10-15 years) can extend service life significantly. Engine controllers and automatic transfer switches may require replacement before generator end-of-life.
Conclusion
Standby generators deliver essential protection against power outages which disrupt operations and create safety risks and lead to substantial financial damage. Your investment will deliver dependable performance during critical times when you learn about power ratings and automatic transfer systems and fuel choices and code requirements.

Regular testing and maintenance ensure reliability; neglect risks failure during emergencies
